# 03 - Where non-drum samples sit: bar, phrase, arrangement

Research note for mindstream. Scope: samples that are NOT the drum break (voices, chops, stabs, pads,
effects, found sound, sampled bass hits) in sample-based dance music, and where skilled producers put
them. Breaks, the Amen, basslines and music cognition are covered in other notes.

Conventions used throughout:

- A bar is 16 steps numbered **0-15**. Step 0 is "the one"; beats fall on 0, 4, 8, 12. In a
  jungle two-step the snare is on 4 and 12 and the kick on 0 and 10 (this matches the measured
  reference in [1][2]).
- Phrase bars are numbered **1-8** or **1-16**.
- Markers: **[measured]** = from the local reference analysis [1][2]; **[snippet]** = taken from a
  search-result summary because the page could not be fetched; **[computed]** = my arithmetic;
  **[unsourced]** = my belief, not found in a source; **[weak]** = from a generic how-to site or a
  forum rather than a named producer, magazine or scholar.
- Pages were read as extracted text. Exact wording was not checked against the originals, so
  everything here is paraphrase; there are no direct quotations from published sources (single-word
  labels aside).
- No lyrics are reproduced. Sampled phrases are described, not quoted.

---

## Summary

The complaint "legible because it's repetitive" describes a system that has solved the bar and not
the phrase. Everything found here points the same way: in the records and sets studied, a non-drum
sample is rarely a per-bar pattern. It is an **event with a job**, placed against the **phrase**, and
most of the time it is **absent**.

The strongest findings:

1. **Samples are sparse and short.** In the measured jungle reference a hook sounds in 39% of bars
   (3.1 of every 8), about 2.6 events per 8 bars, median length 1.2 beats, median silence between
   events 1.5 bars, and nothing plays every bar for 16 bars [1]. Footwork is the deliberate
   exception: one 1-2 beat vocal fragment repeated until it becomes rhythm [17][44][46].
2. **Position in the bar is defined against the drums, not on a free grid.** Stabs are written into
   the steps the main drum hits leave empty and lean on off-beat sixteenths [3]; in the reference
   the high accents sit in the back half of the bar (58%), peaking on steps 10 and 12, while the low
   end takes the front [1]. Samples are placed where the low-end pattern is thinner, not where the
   bass is quieter [1].
3. **Position in the phrase carries the meaning.** Entries happen on 8- and 16-bar boundaries
   [4][5][8]; variation is spent on bar 4 (small) and bar 8 (larger) [3]; fills and stutters land on
   the last one or two bars of a phrase [5][39]; a spoken cue or vocal line gets the last bar before a
   drop with the drums cut under it [5][6]; hook onsets in the reference cluster on bars 4, 5 and 8 of
   8 [1].
4. **Withholding is the main development tool.** The lead vocal of a 1991 techno classic does not
   appear until bar 81 [7]; a 2020s hardcore-jungle remix holds its vocal back for 48 bars [5]; two
   vocal loops in a sample-house track alternate and never overlap [10]; a footwork track reveals its
   phrase a little at a time [19].
5. **A placement habit is held for a section and changed at the boundary** [1]. This, more than any
   single rule, is what separates "intended" from "random": the listener learns where the sample
   lives, then it moves.
6. **Register and space are narrow and consistent.** Hook fundamentals 233-392 Hz, a 10 dB valley at
   about 233 Hz separating them from the bass, nothing below 150 Hz in the sides, delays of an
   eighth-triplet, an eighth and a 45 ms slap, nothing a quarter note or longer [1][2].
7. **Few sources, each with a role, taking turns.** One hook, one punctuation, one bed is typical;
   dense collage (hip hop's layered style, big beat, breakcore) is a named style, not the default
   [4][29][35][52].

What this means for a slice-placing program: stop choosing a step per slice per bar. Choose a **role**
for each source, a **template** per section, a **bar mask** over the 8-bar phrase, and a **budget**
of events; then leave most bars empty.

How well this is sourced: the quantitative backbone is one measured 21-minute jungle set [1][2] plus
bar-numbered arrangement breakdowns of seven records from one magazine series [4]-[10]. Producer
statements (RP Boo, Todd Edwards, Rashad, Burial, Shocklee, Norman Cook, Liam Howlett, Loefah, Mala,
Tim Reaper) support the principles but almost never give step numbers. Step-level placement for
ragga vocals, sirens, airhorns and timestretched vocals in 1991-96 jungle was **not found** in any
citable source; those rows are marked [unsourced] or [weak].

## 1. Position in the bar

### What the measured reference shows [1][2]

- Step occupancy across all onsets: beats (0, 4, 8, 12) 0.83-0.87; the other even sixteenths
  (2, 6, 10, 14) 0.67; odd sixteenths 0.34. Placement is quantised and strongly weighted to eighths.
- The sub attacks at the **front** of the bar (57% of sub onsets in the first half, peaking on step 0
  and step 6). High accents sit at the **back** (58% in the second half, peaking on steps 10 and 12).
  The tested idea of "high call at the front, sub answer at the back" was not supported; the bar
  leans the other way.
- Hooks are **not** placed by low-end level (correlation with sub energy about zero). What changes
  under a hook is low-end *activity*: low-band onset flux falls 2.1 dB. The hook goes in a bar, or a
  part of a bar, where the kick and bass pattern is thinner.
- No answering lag between hook and sub was found at any lag; they do not avoid overlapping.

### What tutorials and breakdowns say

- **Stabs go in the gaps the drums leave.** A magazine walkthrough on chord stabs builds its pattern
  by leaving out the steps occupied by the main drum hits (it names the first and fourth downbeats)
  and emphasising off-beat sixteenths, mixing short hits with a few sustained ones so that the stabs
  answer each other [3].
- **Rave stab and piano riffs** run in eighth and sixteenth notes across two or more octaves, a
  single sampled chord played up and down the keyboard [16][12]. A general reference definition of
  the stab puts it on the downbeat as punctuation [38]. Both are true: the *riff* is syncopated, the
  *single stab* marks the one.
- **A sixteenth-note single-pitch stab line** can run for 8 bars as an "insistence" layer and then
  fill the gaps when kick and bass are muted [8].
- **Vocal stabs in boom bap**: one how-to places them on off-beats or at the end of a bar, and in
  the moments where the beat drops out [34] [weak]. A producer profile of DJ Premier says he
  reconfigures phrases rhythmically to fit the drums and treats the gaps between chops as
  equally important, in two- or four-bar sequences [33].
- **Jungle vocal tags**: a generic songwriting guide puts short hooks on downbeats or before
  drops, uses vocals as tags ahead of drops, and keeps short chants inside the drops [75] [snippet,
  weak]. Another generic guide puts reggae horn
  stabs on the off-beat of bar 2, or as two-note figures echoing the bassline [47] [weak].
- **2-step garage**: the vocal is cut into staccato pieces that interlock with the groove and add
  syncopations of their own, so the singer functions as part of the drum kit [23]. The grid itself is
  swung (about 68-69% on an MPC-style swing at 124-132 BPM) [15], and Todd Edwards quantised his
  micro-samples to a triplet shuffle [18].
- **Footwork**: the sample can *be* the rhythm; RP Boo says that when it is, he puts nothing else
  behind it [17]. A scholarly article describes an RP Boo track in which the sample is displaced
  across bar positions, leaving blank space that the dancer fills [20].
- **Hip hop's layered style**: Hank Shocklee is reported to have placed elements ahead of or behind
  the beat on purpose, for ease or for tension [29], and to have preferred parts played by hand
  over sequencing so that timing varied slightly [28].
- **Dilla**: offsets are small, deliberate and identical on every repeat: claps a little early,
  some hats late, a snare a thirty-second note early, straight and swung layers stacked [30]. At
  166 BPM a thirty-second is 45 ms [computed], the same size as the slapback measured in the
  reference [2]; that is a coincidence of scale, not a sourced link.

### Call and answer

- With the **drums**: supported. Stabs avoid the main hits [3]; samples sit where low-end onsets thin
  out [1]; garage chops interlock with the kit [23].
- With the **bass**: weakly supported. A technique tutorial builds call and response by copying one
  rhythm to a second, contrasting sound and muting most notes so each speaks in the other's gaps
  [55]. The measured reference found **no** hook-then-bass answering pattern [1]. Treat "answer the
  bass" as a taste option, not a genre rule.
- **Sample with sample**: supported. Two vocal loops alternating and never sounding together [10];
  long and short stabs answering each other [3]; a later vocal snippet echoing the opening dialogue
  [9].

### Not found

Step-accurate placement of ragga chat, MC lines or film dialogue in 1991-96 jungle. My belief
[unsourced]: long spoken or toasted phrases are laid free over the bar starting on step 0 or as a
pickup from about step 12-14 of the previous bar, and it is the *end* of the phrase, not the start,
that is aligned (to the drop or to the last snare).

## 2. Position in the phrase

### Entries land on boundaries

- A 1992 hardcore single at 148 BPM: strings alone bars 1-8; a pitched-up rap vocal and the first
  lead line at bar 9; first break and a stab at bar 17 with crashes on bars 17 and 21; second break
  at bar 25; a two-bar breath at bars 33-34 with the strings fading and a crash; the reggae vocal
  chorus from bar 35. Later a drum dropout brings the rap vocal back, and a fourth melodic sample
  enters around bar 71 [4]. Every new sample arrives on an 8-bar line [computed from 4].
- A 2020s hardcore-jungle remix at 160 BPM is built in 16-bar sections. The vocal does not enter
  until bar 49; a pitch-bent vocal snippet appears at bar 78; the chorus line is stated once in the
  next section; a full verse arrives at bar 97; the break stops one bar early at bar 112 so the
  lyric sits alone; a reverse-reverb vocal snippet is used as the riser later on [5].
- French filter house: nearly everything is present from the start but band-limited (highs and lows
  filtered away); turnarounds are mainly 8 bars with occasional 4-bar ones to break expectation; the
  bass ends a bar before a turnaround to signal it [8].

### The last bar, and the last two

- Amen fills at bars 39 and 47 of a 33-48 section, that is bars 7 and 15 of 16 [5] [computed].
- In one filter-house classic the sample is cut into quarter-beat stutters at the end of every
  eighth bar [41] [snippet]; another source says the stuttered chopping lives in the intro and gives
  way to an untouched four-bar loop [40].
- The 8-bar stab progression in [3] changes bar 4 slightly and bar 8 more, using the first two
  sixteenths to lead back to the top.
- A rave-revival track at 132 BPM puts a short spoken cue, cut in sharply with a metallic reverb, on
  bar 64, the last bar before the drop; bar 65 is a one-bar fake-out and the drop is bar 66 [6].
- One jungle guide sweeps a filter across bars 7-8 and then removes the melodic parts entirely for
  bars 9-16 [45] [weak].

### What the reference measured [1][2]

- Hook onsets drift late in the 8-bar phrase: 55% fall in bars 5-8, busiest bars 4, 5 and 8.
- When a shape recurs, the spacing is a median 3.6 bars; layer self-similarity peaks at lags of 2, 4
  and 8 bars. Runs are short: median one appearance before the shape changes, 75th percentile two.
- Stabs are part of the set-up in only 18% of drops, filter sweeps 23%, rolls 27%, risers 0%.
  82% of drops are prepared by taking the low end away while the break keeps running. The gap before
  a drop, when there is one, is about one sixteenth (80 ms).

### Signature placements, and how well each is supported

| placement | support |
|---|---|
| Spoken/vocal cue in the last 1-2 bars before the drop, drums cut | [5][6][4]; advice in [75] [snippet, weak] |
| Dialogue or vocal over a drumless intro, then break | [4][9]; the 1994 ragga-jungle hit opens on film dialogue and horns [49] |
| Impact (crash, noise burst) on bar 1 of a section | [4][5] |
| Single stab or shout on the one of the drop | [38] in general terms; [6] has a lone shout later in the drop; otherwise [unsourced] |
| Chant answering every fourth bar | Not found as a stated rule. Consistent with 4-bar recurrence in [1] and bar-4 variation in [3]. [unsourced] as a named convention |
| Break stops one bar early for the vocal | [5] |
| Vocal returns after a drum dropout mid-track | [4] |
| Timestretched vocal as the pre-drop gesture | Technique documented [50] [snippet], placement [unsourced] |

Standard section lengths, for scale: DJ intro 16-32 bars, drops 16-64 bars often in 32-bar halves,
breakdowns 8-32 bars [43][47][48] [weak]. A dubstep bass drop commonly follows bar 16 or 32 [27].
Tim Reaper's point is worth holding against these templates: jungle does not have to do a given
thing within 32 bars [56].

## 3. Length and density

### Measured reference [1]

| quantity | value |
|---|---|
| bars carrying any melodic or vocal event | 39% (3.1 in 8); per-section range 21-58% |
| events per 8 bars | 2.6 (range 1.3-5.2) |
| event length | median 1.2 beats = about 5 steps [computed]; quartiles 0.8-1.9 beats; 81% under 2 beats; 3% reach a bar |
| longest single event in 1260 s | 2.8 s (7.6 beats) |
| gap between events | median 1.5 bars; 41% over 2 bars; 17% over 4 |
| share of runtime | 12% |
| shapes that ever recur | 39%; a recurring shape returns about 3 times |
| event type | 79% stabs or short fragments; 3% sustained tones or lead lines |

A continuous pad is not counted in these numbers: the analysis treats a constant bed as baseline, and
notes that the ear does too [1]. That is the working distinction between hook and texture:

- **Hook / punctuation**: 1-8 steps long, 2-5 per 8 bars, present in about 3 of 8 bars, changes
  identity often.
- **Texture / bed**: bars to whole sections long, low level, no onsets worth counting; it is the
  thing the hooks are heard *against*. Examples: reverb-soaked bell and beach ambience running under
  whole sections [5]; rain and crackle laid over a track to veil it [25]; nature-style atmosphere
  under break-led tracks [53].

### Other figures

- Footwork: phrases of about 1-4 beats, most often 1-2 beats, occasionally an eighth or a
  sixteenth, looped continuously [46] [weak]; another guide gives the melodic loop as 1-2 bars, 4 at
  most, and main sections of 64-128 bars with little variation [44] [weak]; a forum poster puts short 1-2 beat repeated vocals
  in nine out of ten juke tracks [57] [weak]. Density here is near 100% of bars by design.
- UK garage micro-sampling: 50-75 separate vocal fragments in a track, sometimes over 100 [18].
- Layered hip hop: at least ten distinct samples inside a four-second texture, each under a second,
  the whole then repeated as a larger loop [29].
- Boom bap: sequences of two or four bars [33]; Donuts tracks run 1-1.5 minutes and use
  eighth-note slices, short vocal phrases and gasps [31].
- Dub: the vocal may appear only a couple of times in a whole version [42].
- Sample house: an 8-bar vocal hook is suggested as the unit [51] [weak]; the vocal loop in [10]
  runs in 24-bar turns.

## 4. Transformation

**Pitch.**
- Up, for hardcore: voices sped up to a shriek, pushed past expression into pure urgency [21].
  Norman Cook pitched a soul vocal up by playing the record fast, about three semitones and 20 BPM
  up overall [35]. The 1994 ragga hit's vocal was dropped by accident into a channel pitched up and
  happened to land in key [49] [snippet].
- Down, for menace or weight: horror dialogue pitched down in darkcore [47] [weak]; a whispered
  vocal triggered below its root key so it plays unnaturally slow, then triggered again slightly
  lower as a two-note figure [7]. Burial pitched female voices down and male voices up [25].
- Limits: Todd Edwards kept fragments within about three semitones of the working key because
  quality collapses beyond that [18]; general guides say the same [47][51]. When a sampled chord is
  played lower it also plays slower and rings longer, which is part of the classic stab sound [12].
- Footwork: sources disagree. One says samples are pitched up or down freely [58]; another says
  the tradition avoids pitch-shifting and only matches tempo [44] [weak]. RP Boo describes slowing
  a source down to find what is in it [17].

**Timestretch as an effect.** Timestretching was adopted in jungle so that vocals and strings could
fit a fast tempo without the chipmunk effect [22]; pushed past its limits on the Akai samplers it
gives the metallic, stuttering stretch that became a signature [50] [snippet]. In speed garage, ragga
chants were stretched until they audibly buckled [23]. In big beat, each vocal line was stretched
separately and the warble kept [35]; one hit slows its vocal to a halt mid-track before the finale
[59] [snippet]. A 2020s remix uses audibly timestretched breaks throughout as period colour [5].

**Stutter and retrigger.**
- The stutter edit repeats a small fragment at sixteenth notes by default, also 32nds and 64ths,
  usually occupying an eighth or sixteenth of an otherwise normal bar, at the start or at a
  syncopated end-of-bar position [39].
- Hardware version: Cook moved a sampler loop point by tiny amounts until the held note stuttered
  the way he wanted [35].
- Footwork: a fragment repeated in runs until the words stop meaning anything [46] [weak];
  percussion on a triplet grid at 160 BPM [46][44]. At 160 BPM an eighth-triplet is 125 ms and a
  sixteenth 94 ms [computed].
- Stripped echo: repeat the note at falling volume (about 75%, 50%, 25%) instead of using a delay
  unit [16].

**Chopping into syllables and re-ordering.** Slice to the syllable, load the slices on keys or pads,
and play a new sequence [11]. Pitch first and slice second, or timing changes with pitch [11].
Burial cut a cappellas into new sentences that did not need to make literal sense [25]. Schloss's
definitions: chopping divides a phrase and reconfigures it; looping repeats a longer phrase mostly
unaltered; and a loop always joins the end of a phrase to its beginning in a way the player never
intended [32].

**Off-grid placement.** See Dilla and Shocklee in section 1 [30][29][28]. Burial avoided the
sequencer grid because he could hear it in the result [25]. The reference set is the counter-case:
straight, quantised placement with human content inside the samples [2].

**Reversing.** A reversed vocal or reverse-reverb swell leads *into* an event and should grow as it
goes [51]; it is used on the first word or syllable and before drops [60] [snippet]; in [5] a
reverse-reverb vocal snippet stands in for a riser.

**Filtering over time.** Start band-limited and open: a vocal loop introduced with the filter held
low for 48 bars, then filters bypassed at the first drop [10]; all elements present but with top and
bottom removed in the first bars [8]; an acid line whose cutoff opens across a breakdown [6].

**Gating to the rhythm.** Reverb printed onto a stab, resampled, and the tail cut off abruptly [16];
vocals sidechained to the beat [6]; the melodic bus ducked from kick and snare [45] [weak]. Note the
reference shows no ducking of the *bass* [2].

**Voice into texture.** Heavy reverb, resampling and filtering turn a vocal into a pad that still
reads as human [53]; a two-syllable sample under a long reverb becomes a bed [47] [weak].

## 5. Development over a track

A ladder that recurs across the breakdowns:

1. **Tease** - filtered, distant, or only a fragment. Filter held low on the vocal loop [10]; a
   reverb-soaked, sidechained "ooh" from bar 1 with no other vocal [6]; a single pitch-bent snippet
   two bars before a section ends [5].
2. **State whole** - once, cleanly. The chorus line plays a single time in its section [5]; the
   main vocal's first melodic appearance at bar 19 after a spoken opening [9]; filters bypassed [10].
3. **Chop / repeat** - the stated phrase becomes material. Rashad's track gives a fragment first and
   uncovers the full phrase gradually [19] (the same ladder run in the other direction).
4. **Answer** - a second sample alternates with the first [10]; a late snippet echoes the opening
   dialogue [9]; a filtered variant returns at bar 44 [9].
5. **Take away** - the vocal drops out when the synth line reopens [6]; melodic parts removed for 8
   bars [45]; a guitar sample enters early, leaves, and is glimpsed once more near the end [9]; a
   drum break used exactly once in the whole track [8].
6. **Return changed** - tape echo and volume swells on the chorus [5]; the vocal back but pushed
   further into the mix [6]; the rap vocal returning over a drum dropout [4].

How many distinct samples, and how they take turns:

- Footwork: one or two sources carry a track [17][46].
- Jungle, hardcore, sample house: roughly four to eight non-drum sources across a track, two to four
  sounding in any section, new ones arriving on 8-bar lines [4][5][9] [computed from the breakdowns].
  One guide expects at least two vocal types, a ragga one and a soul one [snippet, weak; the page
  behind the search summary was not identified].
- Big beat: more than ten samples in a single, with the vocal the only recognisable one and the
  rest fragmented beyond recognition [59][35].
- Layered hip hop: ten in four seconds [29], but the team stressed that patterns change and evolve
  through the track rather than sitting still [61].
- UK garage micro-sampling: 50-100 fragments heard as one instrument [18].
- Liam Howlett avoided cycling one 8-bar block; for one song he prepared about ten different 8-bar
  sections and triggered them by hand [36].
- In the reference each sub-section keeps one placement relationship and switches at its edge, and
  the switch lines up with changes of bass riff at the 2- and 4-bar level [1].

## 6. Register and space

**Pitch position (measured, jungle) [1][2].** Hook fundamentals mostly 233-392 Hz (median 277 Hz),
harmonics to about 1.6 kHz, centre of mass about 545 Hz, roughly three octaves above the centre of
the low band. The bass's harmonic energy stops around 174 Hz. Between them is a valley about 10 dB
deep near 233 Hz. There is no separate top-octave hook layer; the break's cymbals own everything
above 2 kHz. When a hook sounds the midrange becomes more *tonal* (harmonic share +0.11), not
brighter.

**Making room.** Isolate or exclude the low end of a sample with steep filters around 186-200 Hz
[14]; in garage, bass-hit and chord samples are high-passed near 100 Hz so the sub layer owns the
bottom [62]; cut the lows on the melodic bus [45]. Do not re-compress material lifted from finished
records; shape with tuning, envelope and EQ instead [14].

**Mono and width.** Reference: nothing below 150 Hz in the sides; 53% of side energy between 400 Hz
and 2 kHz; width arrives with events rather than from a cyclic panner; highs narrow in drops [2].
Producer advice agrees: keep kick, snare, sub and lead centred and let pads, effects and vocals
widen [53]. Examples: a vocal with stereo panning movement [5]; a melodic sample hard-panned left
[4]; a deliberately narrow image with only the clap wandering [8].

**Dry or wet.**
- Jungle reference: tails are short but constant (median T20 about 300 ms); delays are an
  eighth-triplet (about 120 ms), an eighth (181 ms) and a 45 ms slapback; nothing at a quarter note
  or longer [2].
- Generic jungle advice: break nearly dry, vocals on a long plate, stabs on a medium hall, pads very
  long [47] [weak]; short pre-delay keeps words intelligible [75] [snippet, weak].
- Dub and dubstep: delay at an eighth or a dotted eighth, the repeats rolled off above about 5 kHz
  and below about 200 Hz, feedback ridden by hand [13]; about 320 ms is quoted as a dotted eighth at
  140 BPM [63] and 380 ms as a classic setting [42]. A dotted eighth at 166 BPM would be 271 ms
  [computed].
- Throws: send only the last word of a line to a quarter or dotted-eighth delay, 30-50% feedback,
  return filtered (high-pass 100-300 Hz, low-pass 4-8 kHz); slapback 50-120 ms with a single repeat
  [64] [weak].
- The Bomb Squad reportedly used no reverb at all, as a matter of principle [65] [snippet].
- 2-step: the vocal may be left rhythmically intact and changed in *grain* instead: filtered to a
  thin, trebly ghost and set adrift in echo over the bassline [24].
- Burial: the most affecting element is muffled, not foregrounded [25].

## 7. Intentional versus random or loop-like

What producers and writers say, paraphrased:

- **Space is an instrument.** Loefah describes the gaps between drum hits as a part in their own
  right; Mala says a producer can add to a track by removing things, and that with the right
  frequency in place the rest is filler [26].
  Premier's gaps are described as mattering as much as his chops [33]. Footwork's blank space is
  where the dancer goes [20].
- **If the sample is the rhythm, nothing else needs to be there** [17]. One element does one job.
- **Order inside density.** Shocklee insisted the Bomb Squad's sound was not chaos: each structure
  and pattern changes and evolves [61]. Katz hears a ten-sample texture that is then looped as a
  unit, so the density itself has a period [29].
- **Deviation that repeats reads as intent.** Dilla's early claps and late hats are identical every
  time round, which is what turns "sloppy" into a feel [30].
- **Avoid the eight-bar cycle.** Howlett's ten hand-triggered sections [36]; a sample-house track
  described as continuous timbral and rhythmic evolution across about fifteen sections [10]; a
  handful of well-placed sounds that come and go, offered as the jungle ideal [45] [weak].
- **A loop changes what it loops.** Schloss: the join of end to beginning is new material, so even
  a loop is a compositional decision; chopping is valued because it shows the producer's hand [32].
- **Treatment over source.** Tim Reaper: what matters is how samples are treated and processed and
  what is done with them [54]; his rule against sampling jungle to make jungle is about bringing
  something new in [66] [snippet].
- **Chance, but fenced.** One producer recommends controlled randomness in vocal slices and texture
  loops to loosen things without chaos [53].
- **Meaning.** Burial's cut-up sentences did not parse but said what he felt [25]; Rashad picks
  samples people relate to [19]; Cook wants a phrase a crowd can own [35].

Reading these against the measurements, the properties that make placement sound intended are
[inference, each resting on the cited evidence]:

1. **A stable relation to the drums** within a section (same steps, same side of the snare) [1][3].
2. **A phrase-level address**: the event belongs to bar 4, bar 8, the bar before the drop, the first
   bar of a section [1][3][5][6].
3. **Recurrence at 4 or 8 bars, not every bar**, and only a few times [1].
4. **Absence that is long enough to notice** (median 1.5 bars, often 4+) [1].
5. **A change at the boundary** - placement, filter, or which sample speaks [1][10].
6. **One role per source.**

And the properties that make it sound random or looped: every bar filled; the same slice on the same
step every bar; slices landing with equal probability on all sixteen steps; slices covering the
snare; no difference between bar 3 and bar 8; no section in which the sample is missing.

## 8. What is commonly got wrong

1. **Filling every bar.** The reference tops out at 58% of bars in its busiest section; a 32-bar
   section with melodic material in more than 18 bars is busier than anything in it [1].
2. **Bar-level repetition instead of phrase-level.** Three identical bars and a variation is not what
   the reference does: consecutive bars are the *least* similar and identity is carried at 4 bars
   [2].
3. **Uniform step probability.** Odd sixteenths carry about 0.34 occupancy against 0.85 on beats
   [2]. Random step choice sounds random because it is flat.
4. **Covering the main hits.** Stabs on the snare steps mask the one thing the bar depends on [3];
   one producer's advice is to move other parts aside for the snare [53].
5. **Events too long.** Sustained voices in the hook register have almost no precedent in the
   jungle reference (3% of events) [1].
6. **Too high.** Hooks an octave above the 233-392 Hz band read as sweet [2].
7. **Low end left in the sample.** It fills the 185-282 Hz valley and collides with the bass [1][14].
8. **Delays too long for the tempo.** Quarter-note and dotted delays belong to dub and dubstep at
   140, not to jungle at 166 [2][13].
9. **Ducking the bass for the sample.** The reference does not; it thins the low-end pattern [1].
10. **Risers and layered builds in a jungle context.** Zero risers in 22 drops; generic drum & bass
    guides recommend noise risers and 4-8 bar snare rolls [43][48], which is a different idiom.
11. **Over-pitching.** More than about three semitones degrades a vocal unless the damage is the
    point [18][47][51].
12. **Order of operations.** Slice after pitching, not before, if timing matters [11]; tune before
    reverb, delay or resampling smear the signal [53]; do not compress already-mastered samples [14].
13. **Too wet to read.** Excess reverb and delay push a vocal out of audibility [67] [snippet].
14. **Never withholding.** If the hook is present from bar 1 at full strength there is nothing to
    reveal; compare entries at bar 19, 49 and 81 in [9][5][7].
15. **Everything at once.** Two vocal ideas that never overlap sound arranged; two that overlap all
    the time sound like a pile [10].
16. **Treating a texture as a hook or the reverse.** A noisy or continuous slice triggered as short
    stabs sounds like a fault; a short vocal fragment looped as a bed becomes a nagging loop
    [unsourced].

## As rules a program could follow

Goal: place slices of an arbitrary sound against a known drum pattern so that the result reads as
arranged. Inputs per slice: class (**voice-like**, **tonal**, **noisy**, **percussive**), length in
ms, and for the pattern the kick steps K and snare steps S on the 16-step bar. Each rule is tagged
**[well supported]**, **[supported]** or **[inference]**.

### R0. Decide roles before steps [inference from 4, 5, 10, 17]

For each source and each section assign exactly one role:

| role | suited classes | meaning |
|---|---|---|
| `hook` | voice-like, tonal | the one thing the section is about |
| `punct` | tonal, percussive, short voice | stabs and shouts that answer the drums |
| `bed` | noisy, long tonal | continuous texture, not counted as events |
| `fx` | noisy, reversed anything | impacts and lead-ins tied to boundaries |

Limits: at most 1 `hook`, 1-2 `punct`, 1 `bed` sounding in a section; 2-4 non-drum sources per
section; 4-8 per track. A `footwork` mode sets 1 hook, no bed. A `collage` mode lifts the limits.

### R1. Event budget [well supported, 1]

| parameter | default | range seen |
|---|---|---|
| `bars_with_hook_per_8` | 3 | 1.7-4.6 [computed from 21-58%] |
| `hook_events_per_8` | 2.6 | 1.3-5.2 |
| `min_gap_bars` median | 1.5 | 0.3-5.7 (10th-90th) |
| `max_bars_active_per_32` | 18 | - |
| `max_consecutive_identical_bars` | 2 | median 1 |

Draw the bars that carry the hook first; place events only in those bars.

### R2. Bar mask over the phrase [supported, 1, 3, 5, 6]

Weights for which bars of an 8-bar phrase carry `hook` or `punct` events:

`w = [0.6, 0.6, 0.6, 1.5, 1.5, 0.8, 0.9, 1.5]` for bars 1-8 [inference shaped to fit "busiest bars 4,
5, 8; 55% in bars 5-8"].

Additional fixed addresses:
- `fx` impact on step 0 of bar 1 of each section [supported, 4, 5].
- Variation: bar 4 gets a small change to the template, bar 8 a larger one that leads back to bar 1
  [supported, 3].
- Stutter or fill in bar 7 or 8 of 8 (bar 15 or 16 of 16) [supported, 5, 39, 41].
- Pre-drop: last bar (optionally last two) carries one voice-like slice with the drums cut or
  thinned under it; the break may stop one bar early [supported, 5, 6, 4].

### R3. Step weights inside a bar [supported for the shape, inference for the numbers]

Start from the measured occupancy [2] and the "avoid the main hits" rule [3]:

```
base[s]   = 0.85 if s in {0,4,8,12}; 0.67 if s in {2,6,10,14}; 0.34 otherwise
mask[s]   = 0.15 if s in S (snare steps)            # leave the snare alone
            0.40 if s in K and s == 0               # the one belongs to kick and sub...
            1.00 otherwise
half[s]   = 0.85 if s < 8 else 1.15                 # highs at the back of the bar (58/42)
w[s]      = base[s] * mask[s] * half[s]
```

Modifiers:
- `sync` in 0..1 shifts weight from even to odd sixteenths for stab riffs (house and hardcore stabs
  lean on off-beat sixteenths [3][16]). Default 0.2 for jungle, 0.6 for hardcore riffs.
- **After-the-snare slots** (`S+1`, `S+2`) get a bonus of 1.3 for short voice and stab slices
  [inference; consistent with back-of-bar peaks at steps 10 and 12 in 1].
- **On-the-one exception**: for the first bar of a drop or section, `punct` may take step 0 at full
  weight [supported in general terms, 38].
- **Thin-low-end preference**: multiply a bar's chance by 1.25 if its kick-plus-bass onset count is
  below the section median, 0.8 if above. Do not change bass level [well supported, 1].

### R4. Length by class [well supported for the distribution, 1]

| class | role | length |
|---|---|---|
| percussive | punct | 1-2 steps, hard gate |
| tonal | punct (stab) | 1-2 steps; one in four may sustain 3-4 steps [3] |
| tonal | hook | 3-8 steps, median 5 [computed from 1.2 beats] |
| voice-like | hook / punct | 4-8 steps; hard cap 16 steps except the pre-drop slot (up to 32) |
| voice-like, slice longer than 2 beats | - | either truncate to a 1-2 beat fragment, or reserve for intro, breakdown or pre-drop [inference] |
| noisy | bed | 4-32 bars, crossfaded, low level |
| noisy | fx | impact: let it ring 1-4 beats; lead-in: reversed, ending on step 0 |

Voice-like slices should be started on their own transient and ended at a syllable boundary where
one can be detected, never mid-vowel with a hard cut unless stutter is intended [inference].

### R5. Template per section, changed at the boundary [well supported, 1]

Build one template per `(source, section)`: a set of 1-3 step positions plus a bar mask. Reuse it for
the whole section (8, 16 or 32 bars). At the section boundary, redraw. Within a section, vary only by
R2's bar-4 and bar-8 rules and by omission. This is the single most important rule for sounding
intended.

### R6. Recurrence [well supported, 1]

- A given slice-plus-position ("shape") returns at a lag of 4 or 8 bars (2 allowed), about 3 times,
  then is retired or transformed.
- No shape plays in more than 2 consecutive bars (footwork mode excepted).
- About 60% of shapes should be one-offs [computed from "39% recur"].

### R7. Withhold and develop [supported, 5, 7, 9, 10, 19]

Per hook source, schedule stages across sections:

| stage | treatment |
|---|---|
| `tease` | 1 fragment (first 1-2 beats), low-passed or band-limited, wet, -6 dB, at most 1 per 8 bars |
| `state` | the whole slice, dry-ish, once, on a strong address (bar 1 or pre-drop) |
| `chop` | sub-slices on the section template; re-order allowed |
| `answer` | alternate with a second source in 4- or 8-bar turns; never both in the same bar [10] |
| `out` | absent for at least 8 bars |
| `return` | back with one change: echo throw, reverse lead-in, pitch, or further back in the mix |

Parameter `withhold_bars`: 16-48 before `state` (seen: 18, 48, 80).

### R8. Transformations and their triggers

| transform | when | parameters | support |
|---|---|---|---|
| pitch to key | always for tonal and voice | within +/-3 semitones for quality | [18] well supported |
| pitch as effect | style switch | up 3-7 semitones "hardcore"; down 3-12 "menace" | [21][35][7] supported |
| stutter | last 2-4 steps of bar 8 or 16; or pre-drop | retrigger first 1-2 steps of the slice at sixteenths, optionally doubling to 32nds | [39] supported; placement [5][41] |
| footwork retrigger | footwork mode | repeat the slice onset in runs of 3-6 at sixteenth or eighth-triplet spacing; displace the run's start by section | [17][20][46] supported, numbers inference |
| volume echo | after a `punct` hit, sparse | repeats at 75/50/25% level at eighth-triplet or eighth | [16] supported |
| reverse lead-in | before `state`, before a drop | reversed copy ending on the target step, 2-8 steps long, with reverb | [51][5] supported |
| filter opening | `tease` to `state` | low-pass rising over 8-16 bars, bypass at the boundary | [10][8] well supported |
| timestretch effect | pre-drop or breakdown, at most twice per track | stretch the last syllable 2-4x with an old-style grainy algorithm | technique [22][50]; numbers and placement inference |
| re-order syllables | `chop` stage, voice | permute within a 1-bar window; keep the original first syllable first half the time | [11][25]; the "keep first" rule is inference |
| off-grid nudge | hip hop or dubstep modes only | a fixed offset per layer (5-45 ms), the same every bar | [30][29] supported; jungle reference is straight [2] |

### R9. Register and space [well supported for jungle, 1, 2]

- High-pass every non-bass sample at about 200 Hz; keep 185-282 Hz as empty as possible.
- For tonal and voice slices, choose the transposition (within R8 limits) that puts the dominant
  pitch nearest 233-392 Hz. If it cannot get there within 3 semitones, prefer the role `bed` or
  `fx` for that source [inference].
- Level: about 2 dB up on the low band while the event sounds; do not duck the sub.
- Mono below 150 Hz. `bed` and `fx` wide; `hook` centre or near centre; width from short stereo
  delays on events, not from an auto-panner.
- Delay menu for jungle tempo: eighth-triplet, eighth, 45 ms slap; nothing at a quarter or longer.
  Dub / dubstep mode: eighth or dotted eighth, feedback 30-70%, repeats band-passed roughly
  200-400 Hz to 5 kHz, sent only from the last event of a phrase [13][63][64].
- Break dry, voice wetter, bed wettest [47, weak].

### R10. Class-specific defaults

- **voice-like**: role `hook`; fragments of 4-8 steps; pre-drop slot reserved for the longest
  intelligible slice; throws on the final word; intelligibility first (short pre-delay).
- **tonal**: role `punct` as stabs if the slice has a fast attack; role `bed` if slow attack and
  longer than a bar. One sampled chord transposed across 3-5 pitches makes a riff [16][12]; use a
  4-5 note set in the section's key [1].
- **noisy**: role `bed` (continuous, quiet, wide) or `fx` (impact on bar 1, reversed lead-in). Never
  as a per-bar rhythmic hit unless very short.
- **percussive**: role `punct` on weak steps of the back half of the bar as ghost accents, or doubled
  with a tonal stab. Never on S.

### R11. Fenced randomness [supported, 53]

Randomness chooses *among templates* and *which bars are empty*; it does not choose a fresh step per
bar. Seed per section so a section is repeatable.

### What is well supported and what is not

- **Well supported**: the budget (R1), lengths (R4), template-per-section (R5), recurrence (R6),
  register and delay menu (R9), boundary entries and filter opening. These rest on measurement and
  on bar-numbered breakdowns.
- **Supported in principle, numbers mine**: the bar-mask weights (R2), step weights (R3), stage
  schedule (R7), stutter and retrigger settings (R8).
- **Inference only**: after-the-snare bonus, syllable-boundary cutting, the "cannot reach register,
  so make it a bed" rule, timestretch amounts, keeping the first syllable first.
- **Single-reference risk**: R1, R3 and R9 lean on one DJ set by one artist. They describe one good
  answer for 166 BPM jungle, not the whole genre.
