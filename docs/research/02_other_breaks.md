# 02. The other breaks, and how breaks are layered

Written 2026-10-02. Secondary research from the web, built on two earlier briefs in the owner's
corpus. The Amen is covered in a separate report and appears here only where another break is
defined against it.

How to read the marks:

- **[n]** a numbered source in the list at the end. Unmarked means the page itself was read.
- **[snippet]** the claim rests on a search-result summary, not on the page (the site blocked
  fetching, or search ran out). Treat as a lead.
- **[computed]** my own arithmetic from numbers given elsewhere in this file.
- **[unsourced]** my belief or inference; nothing I found supports it directly.
- **[R1] / [R2]** the two earlier briefs: `thelmic/tracks/2026-09-15_jungle_research.md` and
  `thelmic/tracks/2026-09-16_reaper/archetypes_mechanics.md`. Their findings are used, not repeated.

Grids are one bar of sixteen steps, written in four groups of four. Step 1 is the downbeat, so the
backbeats are steps 5 and 13. `x` = hit, `g` = quiet (ghost) hit, `o` = open hat, `.` = rest.

**Limits of this report, stated early.** Web search stopped part-way through (the session's
search allowance was used up), so the later breaks in the catalogue rest on fewer sources than the
earlier ones. WhoSampled, Dogs On Acid, Reddit, Reverb and Gearspace refused to load; anything

35 MB file that could not be fetched. Hit-by-hit grids exist in the sources for seven breaks only;
for the rest the catalogue gives character in words and says so.

## 1. Summary

**Skilled producers do not treat a break as a pattern. They treat it as an instrument with a job.**
Across the sources, each break other than the Amen is picked for one thing it does better than
the others, and it is given a role to match:

1. **A break has a role: anchor, carrier, colour, or switch.** The anchor supplies kick and snare
   weight. The carrier is high-passed and supplies the running sixteenths. Colour is a percussion
   voice (bongos, congas, cowbell) that sits between the drums. A switch is a second anchor held
   back for a new section. The same break can take different roles in different records, but each
   has a usual one. Think and Hot Pants are the standard carriers, named as such by a magazine
   tutorial that suggests laying either one, high-passed, over a beat that lacks energy [22].
   Paradox calls Think "the most versatile one" and credits it with the best tambourine [15].
2. **The measurable difference between an anchor and a carrier is how many of the off-sixteenths
   are filled.** A study of 30 classic breaks found they split into two groups: breaks where the
   "e" and "a" sixteenths are nearly all occupied (87.5-100%: Funky Drummer, Ashley's Roachclip,
   always by a hat or tambourine line), and breaks where they are mostly empty (12.5-62.5%:
   Impeach the President, It's a New Day, Apache, where the only off-sixteenth hits are kicks and
   snares) [1]. The first kind carries; the second kind punctuates. This is the single most
   useful fact here for a program, because it can be measured on any percussive sound.
3. **Swing in these breaks is small, uneven, and not tied to tempo.** Median swing ratio 1.2:1
   (about 54.5% [computed]); it varies inside a bar, and bar 2 of Synthetic Substitution swings
   harder than bar 1 playing the same notes [1]. Which bar a producer loops therefore changes the
   feel. Quantising the same notes removes it and is heard as squarer [1].
4. **Layering is done by splitting the spectrum, not by stacking.** The carrier is high-passed
   somewhere between about 160 and 500 Hz, the anchor keeps the body, and a programmed kick (and
   often snare) sits under both [22][23][24][R1][R2]. The owner's own measurement of a reference
   set found carriers high-passed at 197-308 Hz [R2], inside that range.
5. **Composite breaks are a real practice.** The Tramen (the break in Trace's "Sniper") is an old
   hardcore-record Amen plus Tighten Up plus the Flow break, blended on a mixer with each channel
   overdriven [16][snippet, 46]. The drums of "Dred Bass" are logged as Amen plus Think [snippet,
   48]. Paradox describes morphing parts of several classic breaks into one loop [15].
6. **Switching happens on phrase boundaries.** Swapping the break is listed as one of the standard
   eight-bar turnarounds, with larger changes at 16 and 32 [26][28][R1]. I found no source that
   describes two different breaks answering each other inside one bar as a named technique; the
   nearest evidence is that a single bar is commonly assembled from slices of different bars of
   one break [2], and that composite breaks interleave sources.
7. **Tempo decides how much of the break survives.** At hip-hop tempo the break is looped whole,
   at its own speed, and its swing is the point. By 155-175 BPM it has been raised 5-10 semitones
   [computed], its room sound is trimmed, and it is cut into pieces. Above about 180 it is
   reduced to fragments and distortion.

For mindstream the implication is in section 9: measure three or four things about each slice
source (off-sixteenth occupancy, low-band share, brightness, attack sharpness), assign roles from
those numbers, never let two sources hold the same role in the same band at once, and change
which source holds a role on eight-bar boundaries while one layer stays put.

## 2. Catalogue

Tempos are the original recording's unless stated. "To 170" is the pitch rise needed to reach
170 BPM by speeding up without time-stretching, 12 x log2(170 / tempo) [computed].

### 2.1 Think (Lyn Collins, "Think (About It)", 1972)

- **Source.** Produced by James Brown, drums by John "Jabo" Starks, apparently one take [4][31].
  About 113 BPM [39]; a tutorial that slices it reads 113.93 and takes it to 155 for hardcore
  [29]. To 170: +7.1 st; to 155: +5.3 to +5.5 st [computed]. One bar lasts about 2.1 s
  [computed].
- **It is several breaks, not one.** The record has a "yeah, woo!" break at 1:21, a one-bar break
  at 1:34, another at 2:02, a clean one-bar section at 2:15 and a tambourine loop at 2:21 [4].
  The shout section is the famous one in hip hop; a different section is the one common in
  jungle, drum & bass and breakcore, usually played faster so the shout becomes a recognisable
  squeak [5].
- **Hit by hit.** No fetched source gives a step grid. In words: backbeat snares on 2 and 4, a
  two-sixteenth "double" snare, quiet snare notes on the late sixteenths, and tambourine running
  throughout [snippet, 52]. The swing study left Think out because its swung element is shaken
  percussion with slow attacks, which could not be timed precisely [1]. That exclusion is itself
  the useful fact: the tambourine carries the swing, and it has soft onsets.
- **Character.** Loose, raw, shuffled hats; punchy, tonal snares [4]. Crunchy shuffle, brushy
  percussion, a whippy resonant snare that gives a lurching swing above 160 BPM [8]. Shrill
  tambourine and unmistakable shouts [16].
- **Use by genre.** Hip hop and hip house looped it near its own tempo (Rob Base & DJ E-Z Rock
  "It Takes Two" 1988; Tyree, Fast Eddie) [4][5]. Hardcore pitched it up (2 Bad Mice "Hold It
  Down", 4hero "The Scorcher") [7][8]. Jungle chopped it (Origin Unknown "Valley of the Shadows"
  1993, Foul Play's remix of "Renegade Snares", Remarc "Sound Murderer" and "RIP", Leviticus
  "Burial") [4]. Liquid drum & bass uses a high-passed "Think tops" loop over a programmed
  two-step [25][20]. House and techno producers still use it at 120-135 [4].
- **Role.** Carrier first (tambourine and ghost snares over another break or a programmed kit),
  main break second. The heavily pitched shouts become percussion in their own right [4].
- **Typical treatment.** One list of settings from a drum & bass teacher gives +5 st, a boost
  around 7 kHz and heavy compression [snippet, 45].

### 2.2 Hot Pants (Bobby Byrd, "Hot Pants - I'm Coming, I'm Coming, I'm Coming")

- **Source.** Byrd's 1971-72 answer to James Brown's "Hot Pants"; the version sampled in dance
  music is the 1987 "Bonus Beats" B-side, linked to 892 records on WhoSampled [6]. One pack
  note names Jabo Starks as the drummer, though it describes the James Brown recording [19].
  **Original tempo: not found.** One teacher's settings raise it +6 st [snippet, 45].
- **Hit by hit.** No grid found. A drum loop with tambourine or shaker running over it [6][10].
- **Character.** Tight, sharp snare and crisp top [19]; prominent shaker and snare [23]; one of
  the toughest breaks [16].
- **Use by genre.** Indie-dance ("Fools Gold" uses it) [7]; hardcore (The Prodigy "Charly";
  Bodysnatch "Euphony" 1992, described as simple rolling breaks that defined early darkcore)
  [6][9]; atmospheric drum & bass (LTJ Bukem "Music") [10]; later Adam F "Metropolis" 1996,
  Squarepusher "Come On My Selector" 1997, Coco Bryce "Daktari" 2022 [6]; jump-up [19][28].
- **Role.** Carrier, or main break for a rolling tune. A tutorial uses it purely as a shaker
  layer: everything below 500 Hz removed, sustain trimmed, ducked hard from the kick and snare,
  and widened [23].

### 2.3 Apache (Michael Viner's Incredible Bongo Band, 1973)

- **Source.** Drums Jim Gordon, bongos King Errisson [32]. 117 BPM [1] (118 and 120 are also
  listed [snippet, 49][40]). To 170: +6.5 st [computed].
- **Hit by hit** (four bars, from a published grid [11]; the two hand-drum rows are labelled
  low and high conga there):

  ```
  bar 1   kick      x... .... ..x. ....
  bars 2-4 kick     x.x. .... ..x. ....
  all     snare     .... x... .x.. x..x
  all     hat       x.x. x.x. x.x. x.x.
  bar 1   low drum  x... .... ..x. ....      high drum  .... x..x .x.. x..x
  bar 2   low drum  x.xx .... .... ..x.      high drum  .... x..x .xx. xx..
  bar 3   low drum  ..x. .... .... ....      high drum  x..x x..x .xx. .xx.
  bar 4   low drum  x... ..xx ...x ..x.      high drum  ...x x... .... xx..
  ```

  The kit is plain: eighth-note hats, and only two off-sixteenth hits (steps 10 and 16), which
  matches the measured 25% off-sixteenth occupancy and near-straight swing of 1.1:1 [1]. The
  movement is in the hand drums, which change every bar.
- **Character.** A very wide stereo recording with bongos [16][28]; rounded, bouncy [7];
  galloping and wild [19]. Darker than the Amen, with less cymbal energy above the kick [2].
- **Use by genre.** The founding b-boy break; Sugarhill Gang and many others at original speed
  [32]. In jungle it gives bounce or a lazier contrast (Goldie "Inner City Life"; Dillinja "The
  Angels Fell" 1995) [7][9]. Paradox reports a love/hate relationship with it [15].
- **Role.** Colour (the hand drums, with the low end removed and the bongo band boosted, over
  another beat [23]) or main break for a rolling, percussive tune. Usual rise about +6 st
  [snippet, 45].

### 2.4 Funky Drummer (James Brown, recorded 1969, released 1970)

- **Source.** Clyde Stubblefield; an eight-bar unaccompanied break [33]. 101 BPM [1] (99 in a
  programming tutorial [14]). To 170: +9.0 st; to 160: +8.0 st [computed], in line with a
  quoted working range of +5 to +8 st [snippet, 45].
- **Hit by hit** (two transcriptions agree [11][12]; a third confirms the kick [13]):

  ```
  kick        x.x. ..x. ..x. .x..
  snare       .... x..g .g.g x..g
  closed hat  xxxx xxx. xxxx x.xx
  open hat    .... ...o .... .o..
  ```

  Sixteenth-note hats played with one hand, two open hats (the second lands with the kick on
  step 14), loud snares on 2 and 4 and quiet ones between [13][14].
- **Feel.** Every off-sixteenth is filled (100%) but the swing is almost nil (1.1:1) [1]. Beat 2
  arrives late by about 2.8% of a beat [1]. A dense, straight, quiet-note carrier line with a
  backbeat that sits back.
- **Character.** Roomy, light swing [16]; tight hats and dusty ghost notes [19].
- **Use by genre.** The hip-hop loop of 1986-90 [33]. Baggy and breakbeat house at near-original
  speed [8]. In jungle it is sped up and combined with other breaks (Rufige Kru "Terminator"
  1992, cited as the first pitched and time-stretched drum loops) [8][9]; commonly layered under
  an Amen [28]; liquid and minimal drum & bass [19].
- **Role.** Carrier or second layer (its hats and ghost snares fill the gaps in a sparser
  anchor); main break in hip hop.

### 2.5 Cold Sweat (James Brown, 1967)

- **Source.** Stubblefield. 112 BPM [41] (113 in [R1]). To 170: +7.2 st [computed]. Also called
  "Scotty" [6b].
- **Hit by hit** (two sources agree [12][41]):

  ```
  bar 1  kick   x... .... x.x. ....      snare  .... x..x .... ..x.
  bar 2  kick   ..x. .... x.x. ..x.      snare  .x.. x..x .x.. x...
  both   hat    eighths, open on steps 3 and 11
  ```

  Bar 1 has no snare on beat 4; it lands two steps late, on 15. Bar 2 starts without a kick on
  the downbeat. The displaced backbeat is the break's identity.
- **Character.** Airy and cymbal-heavy with off-beat snares [16]; shuffling hats, less brash
  than Amen or Think [18].
- **Use.** Hip hop; late-90s drum & bass, where the rolling hats suit the texture [snippet, 44]
  (Reprazent "Brown Paper Bag") [6b][10].
- **Role.** Switch-up or main break for a looser section, because its snare placement differs
  from the 5-and-13 norm. Layered under pads or modern snares [18].

### 2.6 Soul Pride (James Brown, recorded 1968, released 1969)

- Stubblefield again [34]. Frenetic and roomy, with several different sections worth sampling
  [16]. Logged in more than 400 records [snippet, 53]; Reprazent "Hot Stuff" [6b]; named with
  Hot Pants and Apache as a staple loop [20][snippet, 42]. **Tempo and grid: not found.** Role by its
  description: a busy main break or fill source. [The role is my inference.]

### 2.7 Tighten Up (James Brown, live in Dallas 1968; released 1998)

- Skittish drums; usually combined with other breaks [7]. Also called "Samurai", because the
  version producers used came second-hand from Photek's "Seven Samurai" [6b][snippet, 46].
  It is one of the three parts of the Tramen (2.20). In one rebuild it was raised a semitone
  [16]. **Tempo and grid: not found.** Role: a top-and-ghost layer inside a composite.

### 2.8 Scorpio (Dennis Coffey & The Detroit Guitar Band, 1971)

- **Source.** Two drummers (Uriel Jones, Richard Allen), Eddie Brown on congas, Jack Ashford on
  tambourine; the break runs 24 bars, then another minute and a half with bass [17]. 118 BPM
  [snippet, 49]. To 170: +6.3 st; to 175: +6.8 st [computed].
- **Character.** A wide stereo mix with hats in one channel and kick and snare in the other
  [17]. That makes it easy to pull the top apart from the bottom and risky to sum to mono.
  [The second sentence is my inference.]
- **Use.** Hip hop in the 1980s; house in 1990 (the conga part alone, in Moby's "Mobility");
  early hardcore and jungle from 1992; then the Bristol producers, who worked on making it sit
  at 175 BPM (Roni Size & DJ Die "Music Box" 1994, Die "Drop Bear") [17]. Krust's account is
  that it worked on dancers without their knowing why, and was preferred because it hit hard
  without dominating the track [17]. Later drumfunk edits (Paradox "Scorpius" 2013) and a
  footwork-leaning rework [17].
- **Role.** A roller's main break, or a subtle under-layer [17]. Colour from the conga part.

### 2.9 Assembly Line (The Commodores, 1974)

- Group vocal shouts and a pinging ride [16]; whip-crack drum fills; suits smoother drum & bass
  (Future Cut "Obsession") [7][6b]. Usual rise +5 or +6 st [snippet, 45]. **Tempo and grid: not
  found.** Role: fills, and a ride-carrying layer. [Role is my inference from the description.]

### 2.10 Do the Do (Kurtis Blow, 1981)

- An electronic-sounding loop from a hip-hop record rather than a funk drummer [10]; heavy on
  rides and claps [snippet, 47]. Also known as "Circles" and "Horizons" after the tunes that
  made it famous (Adam F "Circles", LTJ Bukem "Horizons"), and it is the break in Sound of the
  Future's "The Lighter" [6b][10]. This is the "Lighter-style" break of the brief: a ride-led
  roller of 1994-95. **Tempo and grid: not found.**

### 2.11 Humpty Dump (The Vibrettes)

- Chunky, close to distorted, with a roomy kick [16]. Raised as much as +11 st [snippet, 45].
  Ray Keith "Babylon 5 (Yes Yes)" [6b]. **Tempo and grid: not found.** Role: a heavy, already
  dirty anchor.

### 2.12 Sesame Street (Blowfly, 1974-75), and the Helicopter break

- The source of the drums in Deep Blue's "The Helicopter Tune" (Moving Shadow, 1993) [43]
  [snippet, 44]. In jungle the break is known as "Helicopter", "Paris" or "Pleader" [6b].
- The version people sample is second-generation: forum listeners identify extra hats and
  cymbals, added bongos, pitched-up drums and a snappier sound compared with the Blowfly
  original, with the extra parts taken from other tracks on the same 12-inch [43]. In the tune
  itself the drums back a conga loop whose pitch keeps shifting, which gives the helicopter
  effect [snippet, 44].
- Sampled onward by many ragga-jungle records (Conquering Lion, Congo Natty, X Project) [43].
- **So "Helicopter" is already a layered break**: a funk loop plus added tops plus pitched
  percussion. **Tempo and grid: not found.**

- A regular cowbell and an airy sound [16]. Origin Unknown "Lunar Bass", Friction & K-Tee

### 2.14 Funky Mule (Ike Turner & The Kings of Rhythm, 1969)

- A swinging pattern [15]; funk and punch [30]. Darker than the Amen: less cymbal presence above
  the kick, to the point that a classifier separates the two on kick-band timbre alone [2].
  4hero "Escape That" [6b]; Source Direct "Call & Response", Trace & Nico "Cells", Spirit
  "Siren" [snippet, 51]. One of the three breaks researchers judged most prominent in hardcore,
  jungle and drum & bass, with Amen and Apache [2]. **Tempo and grid: not found.**
- Role: a main break for darker, swung material; the alternative anchor to the Amen.

### 2.17 The hip-hop canon: Impeach the President, Ashley's Roachclip, Synthetic Substitution, It's a New Day

These are sparse kick-and-snare breaks. They matter here as the opposite pole to the carriers.
Tempo, swing and off-sixteenth occupancy are from [1]; grids from [11], checked against [12].

| break | BPM | swing ratio (max) | off-16ths filled | to 170 [computed] |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Impeach the President (Honey Drippers, 1973) | 95 | 1.4 (1.5) | 12.5% | +10.1 st |
| Ashley's Roachclip (Soul Searchers, 1974) | 98 | 1.3 (1.4) | 100% | +9.5 st |
| Synthetic Substitution (Melvin Bliss, 1973) | 96 | 1.3 (1.7) | 50% | +9.9 st |
| It's a New Day (Skull Snaps, 1973) | 95 | 1.7 (1.7) | 25% | +10.1 st |

```
Impeach        kick  x... ...x x... ..x.   snare .... x... .... x...   hat x.x. x.xx x... x.x.  open hat step 11
Roachclip      kick  x.x. ..x. .xx. ....   snare .... x... .... x...   hat x.x. x.x. x... x.x.  open hat step 11
               tambourine on all sixteen steps, accented on the odd ones
Synthetic Sub  kick  x.x. ...x .xxx ...x   snare .... x... .... x...   hat eighths (bar 2: open on step 3)
New Day        kick  x.x. .... ..xx ...x   snare .... x... .... x...   hat eighths
```

- **Impeach.** One swung hit, the kick on step 8, pulls into beat 3. The hat swings at about
  1.2 while that kick swings at about 1.5, and the study argues that mismatch is the break's
  character [1]. The first break to be sampled and re-triggered in hip hop (Marley Marl, 1986)
  [35].
- **Roachclip.** The same kind of kit pattern with a sixteenth-note tambourine laid over it
  [1][11]. It is a carrier and an anchor in one recording, which is why it loops so well alone
  ("Paid in Full", "Unbelievable", "Set Adrift on Memory Bliss") [36]. The second grid source
  puts the late kicks on 9-10 rather than 10-11 [12]; the two disagree.
- **Synthetic Substitution.** Bernard Purdie; a two-bar intro break in over 800 records [37].
  Bar 2 repeats bar 1 with more swing, and records that loop bar 2 inherit the bounce [1].
- **It's a New Day.** The swung kicks on 12 and 16 pull into beat 4 and into the next downbeat,
  then let go [1].
- **In jungle** they turn up in techstep and Bristol records (Ed Rush & Optical "Pac Man" uses
  It's a New Day) [6b], but they are hip-hop breaks first. Raised ten semitones they keep little
  of their weight. [The last sentence is my inference.]

### 2.18 Composite and second-generation breaks: Sniper / Tramen, Dred Bass, Lighter, Bulldog

- **Tramen ("Sniper", "Firefight").** Built by Dom & Roland; first heard on Trace's "Mutant
  Revisited" (1996) and sampled from the clean intro of "Sniper" (1999) [snippet, 44][6b]. Its
  parts are the Amen, Tighten Up, and the break from Alex Reece's remix of Model 500's "The
  Flow" [snippet, 44][snippet, 46]. Dom's own account, as quoted on a forum: an Amen lifted from
  an old hardcore record with a distinct sound, layered with pieces of the other two, about
  sixteen elements through the desk, on a Roland S760 with three mixer channels driven into
  distortion and equalised until they blended [snippet, 46]. He has said the point of having the
  finished composite is not having to solve the phase relationship between three stacked kicks
  or snares each time [16]. One source says "Sniper" combines Tighten Up with Apache instead
  [7]; the weight of evidence is against that.
- **Dred Bass (Dead Dred, 1994).** Known for its reversed bassline [9]. Its drum sources are
  logged as Amen and Think [snippet, 48]. How the roll was programmed: not found.
- **Lighter.** See Do the Do (2.10).
- **Bulldog.** Nothing found under this name.
- **Rolled breaks in general.** 1994 is described as the year of rolling Amens (Nasty Habits
  "Here Come the Drumz"), and of a second break chopped and laid quietly behind the main one
  (Subnation "Scottie's Sub", 1993) [9]. Roll mechanics are in [R1] section 1.

## 3. Layering practice

### 3.1 What is stacked with what, and why

| stack | reason given | source |
|---|---|---|
| Think (or Hot Pants) tops over another break or a weak beat | adds top-end movement and energy | [22] |
| Think over Amen | the classic pairing: one break anchors, chopped high-passed sections of the other run on top | [snippet, 47][26] |
| Think tops over a programmed two-step | organic shuffle on a clean kit (liquid) | [25] |
| Funky Drummer under Amen | looser, funkier ghost notes beneath a hard anchor | [28] |
| Hot Pants shaker over Apache bongos over a plain kick and snare | three bands: kit, bongo mids, shaker top | [23] |
| A ride-heavy break with a break that has no ride | two rides fight; one must own the cymbal | [21] |
| Amen + Tighten Up + Flow (Tramen) | one composite with a new identity | [16][snippet, 46] |
| Second break chopped and placed quietly behind the main one | depth without a second lead | [9] |
| Any break + programmed kick (and often snare) | weight and definition the break lacks | [22][27][R1] |
| A second break only for its hats and ghost notes, 6-8 dB down | fills the holes in the main break's hat line | [28] |

The principle stated most often is **complement, not double**: choose the second break for what
the first lacks, and remove from it whatever the first already supplies [21][24][28].

### 3.2 Filtering one against the other

High-pass corners quoted for the upper layer:

| corner | context | source |
|---|---|---|
| 80-120 Hz | hip hop, only to clear a programmed kick | [38] |
| about 160 Hz | second jungle break, to stop two kicks colliding | [21] |
| 197-308 Hz | measured on a reference jungle set's high-passed carriers | [R2] |
| 214 Hz (with a cut at 303 Hz and a low-pass at 4.84 kHz) | break over a separate kick | [R1] |
| 200-400 Hz, set by the snare's fundamental | forum advice on layering breaks | [snippet, 47] |
| about 500 Hz | Hot Pants used purely as a shaker layer | [23] |
| 700-750 Hz | hats and cymbals in a programmed garage kit | [24b] |

- **"Bracketing".** Each layered loop loses both its bottom and its top so that it occupies a
  band, rather than only being high-passed [24][R1].
- **Trim the tails.** Sustain and room are shortened on layers so they do not fill the gaps
  [23][26]; in a sampler, attack at zero, decay shortened, release off [27].
- **Boost what the layer is there for.** The bongo band on Apache [23]; 7-9 kHz on Think or Hot
  Pants [snippet, 45].
- **Level.** A supporting break sits well under the anchor: 6-8 dB in one guide [28]; in a house
  example a break-derived snare slice sits about 8-10 dB under the other snare layers [24c].
- **Duck the layer.** The shaker layer is side-chained from the kick and snare [23]; a
  band-limited duck of the break from the kick is the modern habit [R2].

### 3.3 Phase and timing

- **Start times.** For stacked hits the opening transient is what matters: zoom in, slide the
  layer so the two either fuse or deliberately flam, and flip polarity if the first half-cycles
  oppose [24a]. Small losses in the opening transient cost the low end badly [24a].
- **Three stacked kicks or snares is a phase problem every time**, which is one reason producers
  bounce a composite and reuse it [16].
- **Old breaks are not on a grid.** Their timing wanders, which makes two of them hard to lock by
  triggering at offsets [27]. Fixes: slice and move the snares forward so they
  land together; slice late kicks separately so the hats are not dragged with them [21]. A late
  snare makes the whole beat feel slower; setting slice points by hand at the true attack fixes
  it [22].
- **Offset on purpose.** Two differently chopped breaks, one shifted a beat or a beat and a half
  against the other (4 or 6 steps), is described as the layered-breaks signature of the old
  sound [R1][27b]. The opposite method, triggering both from the same offsets so they move
  together, gives a thick rolling texture [27].
- **Copy the groove across.** Hat shuffles and kick-hat-snare figures from one break are
  replicated in the other so they agree rather than rub [21]. In hip hop the break's timing is
  extracted and applied to the added one-shots [38].

### 3.4 The programmed kit underneath

- A programmed kick goes under the break and the break's own kick loses its bottom [27][R1].
- Drum-machine hits that follow the break's pattern, mixed low, restore definition [22].
- Sample-pack "fortified" breaks are exactly this: modern hits layered on the old loop [18][19].
- Techstep went further and layered processed hits onto tightly cut fragments [20]; Boymerang's
  "Soul Beat Runna" (1996) took the Amen apart and replaced each hit with a new sample [9].
- Paradox layers single hits onto parts of a break to lift them, and otherwise leaves it bare
  [15].

### 3.5 Mono or stereo

- Keep the break mono and central: Paradox abandoned stereo breaks mid-project because they
  sounded boxed-in [15]; forum and hip-hop guides say the same, and warn against widening the
  drum bus [27][38].
- Width is given to the top layers only: the shaker layer is widened [23]; hats, shakers and
  percussion are panned off-centre [19b].
- Several source recordings are very wide (Apache, Scorpio) [16][17], so their two channels are
  different instruments. [That this argues for choosing one channel or checking the mono sum is
  my inference.]
- An exception as effect: on "Timeless" the break was split into two mono files adjusted
  separately so that it turned through the stereo field [16b].

## 4. Switching between breaks

**What the sources state.**

- **Every 8 bars** a turnaround, and replacing the drums with another break is one of the listed
  options, alongside dropping the drums, filtering, pulling the bass and a reverse crash [26].
  Fills and new sections at 16 and 32 [19b][R1]. A fill tells the listener a change is coming
  [23].
- **At the second drop** the common shape is a second main section with new break variations
  [snippet, 47].
- **By stacking, not replacing.** In the owner's reference set none of 15 drops replaced the
  carrier; full-range material arrived underneath it [R2]. So one layer runs through the change
  and another is swapped.
- **Composition by block switching.** Paradox finds grooves by switching quickly between
  patterns in blocks and keeping the pairs that fold into each other [15]. That is A/B
  alternation at the block level, chosen by ear for how the seam swings.
- **Call and response between two voices** is documented for two rim-shot samples in a garage
  kit, the second pitched up two semitones [24b].

**What I could not find.** A source that names "break A answers break B inside one bar" as a
jungle technique, or one that counts how often classic records change break. The nearest things
are the composite breaks above and the finding that one bar is commonly built from slices of
two different bars of the same break [2].

**What it does for the listener** (kept short; another report covers perception). A change of
break changes timbre, room and swing at once while tempo and backbeat hold, so it marks a
section without breaking the pulse. Holding the carrier across the switch keeps the fast layer
continuous. [Both sentences are my inference, consistent with [R2] and [23].]

| band | which breaks | how cut | pitch [computed] | notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|

| **118-140** hip house, big beat, breakbeat house, nu skool breaks, garage | Think, Hot Pants, Funky Drummer, Apache, Scorpio's congas | loops, often over a four-on-the-floor kick [39c]; break slices used as one layer of a programmed snare [24c] | Think to 130 = +2.4 st | big beat is 100-140 with loud, heavily compressed, distorted breaks [39d]; nu skool breaks 125-140 moved to programmed, technical drums [39e]; garage at 124-132 is programmed with heavy swing (MPC 68-69) and uses no break in the tutorial read [24b] |

Two things follow. First, the same break changes role as tempo rises: Funky Drummer is the main
loop at 100 BPM and a ghost-note under-layer at 170. Second, breaks recorded slower need a
bigger pitch rise, so the 95-BPM canon arrives at jungle tempo thin and bright, while the
112-118 BPM breaks (Think, Cold Sweat, Shack Up, Apache, Scorpio) arrive with more body. [The
second point is my inference from the arithmetic; I found no source that states it.]

## 6. Processing that marks an era

- **SP-1200 (hip hop, 1987 on).** 12-bit at 26.04 kHz; 10 s of memory, 2.5 s per sound; no
  reconstruction filter, so it is bright with imaging; crude pitch-shifting adds distortion
  that people liked [39j]. Records were sampled fast and pitched back down inside the machine
  to save memory, at a cost in fidelity [39j]. Think's sampling took off when this machine
  and the break compilations arrived together in 1987 [4][31].
- **Break compilations.** Ultimate Breaks and Beats (1986-91) supplied most of the canon, some
  already re-pitched (the Amen pressed at the slower speed) [39k]. Many UK producers therefore
  worked from second- or third-generation copies, a point Rob Playford makes about breaks that
  had already been through rough processing [16b]. Helicopter and Tighten-Up-via-"Samurai" are
  cases [43][snippet, 46].
- **Akai S950 / S1000 (hardcore and jungle).** 12-bit with variable bandwidth; time-stretch
  with metallic, stuttering artefacts; details in [R1] and [R2].
- **Deliberate degradation.** Playford: sample the break, add noise, equalise it harshly,
  distort it, to make it sound authentic [16b].
- **Desk distortion.** The Tramen's sound came from overdriven mixer channels, which is hard to
  copy in software [16][snippet, 46].
- **Pitch as an edit.** Whole break up 5-10 st; single slices pitched (last snare up a semitone;
  a slice down 7 st for a mash-up effect) [29]; the break shifted by octaves for extra tones,
  which works best on breaks with ride-cymbal wash to supply noise [15].
- **Room made from the break.** Paradox samples reverb tails and the air between hits and plays
  them back dry as slices, instead of adding reverb afterwards [15].
- **Roll velocity.** -12, -9, -6, -3 dB steps for a rising snare roll [29].
- **Weight tricks.** A long plate reverb low-passed at about 60 Hz under the drums; parallel
  compression blended with the dry drums [22].
- **Big beat.** Loud, heavily compressed, distorted breaks with filter, phaser and flanger
  sweeps [39d].
- **Since 2010.** Granular treatment of transients, resampling through saturation, recording to
  tape for a worn sound [20].

## 7. What is commonly got wrong

1. **Looping a classic break unaltered** at jungle tempo [44b].
2. **Stacking two breaks full-range.** Kicks collide and the low mids fill up [21][19b].
3. **Two rides at once** [21].
4. **Ignoring phase** when hits are stacked; the low end thins [24a].
5. **Late snares left late**, so the beat drags; or everything quantised hard, so the feel is
   lost [22][1][38]. The fix in both cases is moving slices by hand, not snapping.
6. **Time-stretching a break that should have been re-pitched.** Tough breaks go weak when
   stretched to tempo [27].
7. **Leaving the room sound on every layer** [23][26].
8. **Widening the drum bus** or working with stereo breaks whose channels disagree [15][38].
9. **Processing a wrong sample instead of replacing it.** Choose a better source before adding
   plug-ins [22].
10. **Fast double kicks above 160 BPM**, which squash the groove [15].
11. **Adding where cutting would do**, in equalisation [27].
12. **A second break that duplicates the first** instead of filling its gaps [28][21].

## 9. As rules a program could follow

mindstream cuts sound into slices and places them on a sixteenth grid. The sound need not be a
drum break. These rules treat any percussion-like source as the producers above treat a break.
Each rule says how well it is supported: **[supported]** means a source states it or measures
it; **[inference]** means I derived it.

### 9.1 Measure four things about each source

1. **Off-sixteenth occupancy.** Over a bar at the source's own tempo, the share of the eight
   even-numbered sixteenths that contain an onset. This is the study's "swing density" [1].
   [supported as a descriptor that splits the canon in two: 87.5% and above, or 62.5% and below.]
2. **Low-band share.** Fraction of onset energy in roughly 50-325 Hz, the band used to find
   breakbeat kicks; and 430-5000 Hz for the snare band [2]. [supported as band choices]
3. **Brightness.** Energy above about 5 kHz relative to the snare band, or spectral centroid.
   The Amen is brighter, with more cymbal above its kicks, than Apache or Funky Mule [2].
   [supported as a difference between breaks; the 5 kHz split is my choice]
4. **Attack sharpness.** Rise time of the onsets. Shaken percussion (Think's tambourine) has
   gradual attacks, sharp enough to be heard as rhythm and too soft to time precisely [1].
   [supported]

Two more are worth having: **tail length** (time to half level after an onset; the reference
carriers fall to half in 15-30 ms, inside one sixteenth of about 88 ms at 170 BPM [R2][computed])
and **channel difference** (how unlike left and right are).

### 9.2 Give each source a role

| if the source has | role | basis |
|---|---|---|
| low occupancy, high low-band share, sharp attacks | **anchor**: owns steps 1, 5, 13 and the syncopated kicks | [1] describes these breaks; the mapping to a role is [inference] |
| high occupancy, low low-band share, bright | **carrier**: runs the sixteenths, high-passed | [22][25][R2] [supported] |
| high occupancy, soft attacks | **carrier bed**: sits lowest in level, supplies swing without competing transients | [1][23] [inference] |
| mid-band pitched hits with gaps, pattern changing bar to bar | **colour**: placed in the gaps, low end removed, its own band boosted | [11][23] [supported for bongos] |
| dense, bright, sharp, roomy | **fill or switch**: held back, used at phrase ends or for a new section | [inference] |
| both a sparse kit and a steady top in one recording | may stand alone as anchor and carrier | Roachclip [1][11] [inference] |

At any moment exactly one source should hold the anchor role and at most one the carrier role
in a given band. [supported: complement, do not double [21][28]]

### 9.3 Layer two sources

1. **Pick the pair by difference.** Prefer a carrier whose occupancy is high where the anchor's
   is low; reject pairs that both have a ride-like sustained top [21]. [supported]
2. **High-pass the carrier.** Start at 200-300 Hz [R2]; raise toward 500 Hz if it is only there
   for the top [23]; never below about 160 Hz when an anchor is present [21]. Optionally
   low-pass around 5-6 kHz if a separate hat layer exists [R1]. [supported]
3. **Shorten the carrier's tails** so each slice ends before the next sixteenth [23][27][R2].
   [supported]
4. **Set the carrier 6-10 dB under the anchor** [28][24c]. [weakly supported: two sources, one
   of them a general guide]
5. **Thin the carrier where the anchor speaks.** On steps where the anchor has a loud hit,
   either drop the carrier slice or duck it [23]. [supported as ducking; dropping is inference]
6. **Align or offset, never drift.** Either place both from the same step positions so they
   move together [27], or shift the carrier's pattern by a fixed 4 or 6 steps [27b]. For
   coincident hits, align onsets to the sample and check polarity [24a]. [supported]
7. **Keep the source's own micro-timing inside a slice group.** Move slices in blocks of two or
   four steps; do not snap each hit [R1][38]. The swing is uneven within a bar and differs
   between bars of the same break, so which source bar a slice came from matters [1].
   [supported]
8. **Preserve swing ratio when changing tempo, not milliseconds.** Re-pitching and stretching
   both scale delays with the bar. A 1.4:1 swing at 95 BPM is about 26 ms of delay; at 170 it
   is about 15 ms [computed], close to the 10 ms at which swing stops being heard [1]. If the
   feel vanishes at speed, the remedy is to exaggerate the ratio, not to add fixed delay.
   [the arithmetic is mine; the 10 ms threshold is from [1]; the remedy is inference]
9. **Put a plain kick, and if needed a snare, under the anchor** and remove the anchor's own
   bottom [27][22][R1]. [supported]
10. **Anchor mono and central; width only on the carrier and colour** [15][23][38]. If a source's
    channels differ strongly, use one channel or the sum only after checking the sum has not
    lost the kick. [first sentence supported; second inference]

### 9.4 When to switch

1. **Change which source holds a role only on a phrase boundary**: 8 bars for a small change,
   16 or 32 for a new anchor [26][19b][R1]. [supported]
2. **Change one role at a time.** Keep the carrier running when the anchor changes, or the
   reverse. [supported by the reference measurement [R2]; no producer states it]
3. **Announce it.** The bar before a switch carries a fill, a gap or a drop-out [23][26].
   [supported]
4. **Alternate at block level before alternating inside the bar.** Try A A B A, A B A B, and
   A B C D arrangements of two- or four-step blocks from two patterns, and keep the pairs whose
   seam lands a strong hit on a strong step [15]. [supported as a working method; the
   acceptance test is inference]
5. **Within a bar, let a second source answer rather than interrupt**: give it the last 2-4
   steps of a bar or the gaps after the anchor's backbeats. [inference; the only direct support
   is two-voice call and response in a programmed kit [24b] and bars built from slices of
   different source bars [2]]
6. **Make composites and reuse them.** Once two or three sources have been aligned and balanced
   into a bar that works, keep that bar as a new source with its own identity, as the Tramen and
   Helicopter were [16][43]. [supported as practice; proposing it for a program is inference]

### 9.5 Tempo-dependent settings

| output tempo | slice size | how much source order to keep | carrier |
|---|---|---|---|
| 90-110 | half-bar to bar | nearly all | optional; the source's own top is enough |
| 118-140 | beat to half-bar | most | a break as one layer over a programmed kit |
| 145-165 | step pairs to beat | some | on |
| 160-175 | single steps and pairs | little; re-deal each bar | on in most bars (the reference has it in about three-quarters of bars [R2]) |
| 180+ | single steps and shorter | none | optional; fragments carry |

[The left three columns summarise section 5 and are supported in outline; the exact slice sizes
are inference.]

### 9.6 What bears on "legible because it is repetitive"

The catalogue suggests three sources of variety that do not need a new pattern:

- **The bar the slice came from.** Apache's hand drums differ in all four bars; Cold Sweat's two
  bars put the snare in different places; Synthetic Substitution's second bar swings harder
  [11][12][1]. Drawing slices from different source bars for the same grid position changes the
  feel while the grid stays put. [supported as fact about the breaks; the use is inference]
- **The role assignment.** The same two sources with roles swapped give a different section.
  [inference]
- **The second layer's offset.** Shifting the carrier by 4 or 6 steps against the anchor
  [27b] changes every coincidence in the bar without touching either pattern. [supported]
